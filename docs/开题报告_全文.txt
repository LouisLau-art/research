Research Content
Please describe research problems and key challenges to be addressed in the research
Social networks have become one of the most important forms of information infrastructure in the modern era. Platforms such as Facebook, Twitter, and LinkedIn connect billions of users, generating massive graph-structured data that encodes complex social relationships. A fundamental problem in social network analysis is *link prediction*, which aims to infer the likelihood of a future connection between two nodes that are not currently linked. Accurate link prediction has broad applications, including friend recommendation systems, knowledge graph completion, biological protein interaction prediction, and terrorist network identification [5][7].
Traditional approaches to link prediction rely on hand-crafted topological features derived from the network structure. These methods, including Common Neighbors (CN), Jaccard Coefficient, Adamic-Adar Index [6], and Resource Allocation Index [8], compute pairwise similarity scores based on the local neighborhood structure of nodes. While these methods are computationally efficient and interpretable, they suffer from significant limitations. First, they capture only local structural information and fail to incorporate global network topology. Second, their performance degrades substantially in sparse networks where the overlap of neighborhoods is limited [5][7].
In recent years, *network representation learning (also known as network embedding) has emerged as a powerful paradigm for learning low-dimensional, dense vector representations of nodes in a network. Pioneering methods such as DeepWalk [3], Node2Vec [2], and LINE [4] have demonstrated remarkable success in capturing both local and global network structure. DeepWalk employs truncated random walks on the graph and applies the Skip-gram model (originally designed for natural language processing) to learn node embeddings. Node2Vec extends DeepWalk by introducing two hyperparameters, p (return parameter) and q* (in-out parameter), to control the random walk strategy, enabling a flexible trade-off between breadth-first search (BFS) and depth-first search (DFS) exploration of the network [2]. LINE learns node representations by preserving both first-order proximity (direct connections) and second-order proximity (shared neighborhood structure) [4].
However, a critical limitation of these random walk-based methods lies in their *walk generation strategy. In DeepWalk, the transition probability from the current node to a neighboring node is uniformly distributed, treating all neighbors equally regardless of their structural importance. Node2Vec improves upon this by introducing biased random walks, but its bias is determined solely by the walk's previous step (i.e., whether to return, stay local, or move outward), without considering the structural similarity* between the current node and its potential next-hop neighbors. This means that structurally dissimilar neighbors receive the same transition probability as structurally similar ones, potentially leading to suboptimal node embeddings that fail to capture fine-grained local structural patterns.
The *key challenges* addressed in this research are as follows:
1.  *Integration of structural similarity into random walk strategies:* How to effectively incorporate local topological similarity metrics (such as Jaccard Coefficient and Adamic-Adar Index) into the random walk transition probabilities of DeepWalk and Node2Vec, so that the walk is biased toward structurally similar neighbors.
2.  *Balancing local and global information:* The improved random walk must preserve the ability to capture global network structure while simultaneously encoding richer local structural information through similarity-weighted transitions.
3.  *Computational efficiency:* The incorporation of structural similarity metrics must not introduce prohibitive computational overhead, ensuring that the method remains scalable to large-scale social networks.
4.  *Generalizability:* The proposed method should be validated across multiple real-world social network datasets with varying characteristics (e.g., density, clustering coefficient, community structure) to demonstrate its robustness and generalizability.
Please state the significance of the research
The significance of this research is both theoretical and practical.
*From a theoretical perspective*, this research bridges two traditionally separate paradigms in link prediction: topology-based similarity methods and network representation learning methods. By embedding structural similarity information directly into the random walk process, the proposed approach creates a unified framework that leverages the strengths of both paradigms. Traditional similarity metrics such as Jaccard Coefficient and Adamic-Adar Index encode rich local structural information that captures the notion of neighborhood overlap and node importance [6][8]. Network embedding methods such as DeepWalk and Node2Vec, on the other hand, excel at capturing global network patterns through their random walk and neural network training procedures [2][3]. The proposed method synergistically combines these two types of information at the walk generation stage, which is a novel contribution to the field of network representation learning.
*From a methodological perspective*, this research explores a relatively underexplored direction in the network embedding literature. While extensive research has been devoted to improving the neural network architecture and training procedures for network embedding, comparatively less attention has been paid to improving the quality of the input sequences (i.e., the random walks) that feed into these models. This research demonstrates that the walk generation strategy is a critical component that significantly affects the quality of the learned embeddings, and that incorporating domain-specific structural knowledge into the walk strategy can lead to substantial improvements in downstream tasks such as link prediction.
*From a practical perspective*, improved link prediction has direct applications in real-world social network services. More accurate friend recommendation systems can enhance user engagement and platform growth. In academic citation networks such as Cora and Citeseer, better link prediction can facilitate literature discovery and collaborative research. In security applications, improved link prediction can aid in identifying hidden connections in criminal or terrorist networks [7]. The proposed method, by producing higher-quality node embeddings, can serve as a foundational technique for these and other graph-based machine learning tasks.
Furthermore, this research contributes to the growing body of knowledge on how to design better random walk strategies for graph-based machine learning. The insights gained from this work may be transferable to other graph learning tasks beyond link prediction, such as node classification, community detection, and graph generation, thereby having a broader impact on the field of graph mining and analysis.
Please describe research objectives and planned outcomes of the research
The primary *research objectives* of this study are as follows:
1.  *To design and implement an improved random walk strategy* that incorporates local structural similarity metrics into the transition probabilities of network representation learning algorithms. Specifically, the uniform transition probability in DeepWalk and the BFS/DFS-biased probability in Node2Vec will be replaced or augmented with Jaccard Coefficient-weighted transition probabilities, so that the random walker preferentially visits neighbors that share more structural similarity with the current node.
2.  *To generate high-quality node embeddings* using the improved random walk sequences combined with the Skip-gram model (Word2Vec). The hypothesis is that similarity-weighted random walks will produce node sequences that better reflect the local structural patterns of the network, leading to embeddings that more accurately capture node proximity relationships.
3.  *To evaluate the proposed method on the link prediction task* using multiple real-world social network datasets, including the Facebook Social Network, Cora, and Citeseer datasets. The evaluation will employ the Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC) metric, following the standard experimental protocol of randomly hiding 20% of existing edges as the positive test set and sampling an equal number of non-existing edges as the negative test set [8].
4.  *To conduct comprehensive comparative experiments* against both traditional similarity-based methods (Jaccard Coefficient, Adamic-Adar Index, Common Neighbors, Resource Allocation) and state-of-the-art network embedding methods (original DeepWalk, original Node2Vec) to demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed improvement.
The planned *outcomes* of this research include:
-   A novel similarity-weighted random walk algorithm that can be integrated into existing network embedding frameworks. -   Extensive experimental results demonstrating the superiority of the proposed method over baseline approaches on multiple benchmark datasets. -   A detailed analysis of the impact of different structural similarity metrics (Jaccard, Adamic-Adar, etc.) on the quality of random walks and the resulting embeddings. -   A completed master's thesis documenting the entire research process, from problem formulation and literature review to algorithm design, experimental evaluation, and conclusion.
## 2. Proposed Research Plan and Methodology
2. Proposed Research Plan and Methodology
(a) Please describe research methods, the theories and strategies adopted in the research
The proposed research adopts a combination of *network representation learning and structural similarity analysis* as its core methodology. The overall approach consists of three main stages: (1) similarity-weighted random walk generation, (2) node embedding learning via Skip-gram, and (3) link prediction via binary classification.
*Stage 1: Similarity-Weighted Random Walk Generation*
The foundation of this research is the modification of the random walk transition probability. In the standard DeepWalk algorithm [3], given a current node v with neighbors N(v), the transition probability to each neighbor u ∈ N(v) is uniform: P(u \| v) = 1 / \|N(v)\|. This treats all neighbors equally, ignoring their structural relationship with the current node.
In the proposed method, the transition probability is weighted by a *local structural similarity metric*. Taking the Jaccard Coefficient as the primary example, the weighted transition probability is defined as:
P(u \| v) = Jaccard(v, u) / Σ_{w ∈ N(v)} Jaccard(v, w)
where Jaccard(v, u) = \|N(v) ∩ N(u)\| / \|N(v) ∪ N(u)\| measures the overlap between the neighborhoods of nodes v and u. This formulation ensures that the random walker is biased toward neighbors that share a higher proportion of common neighbors with the current node, thereby generating walks that better capture local community structure and structural equivalence.
To handle the edge case where Jaccard(v, u) = 0 for all neighbors (which can occur in tree-like substructures or very sparse regions), a smoothing parameter ε is introduced: P(u \| v) = (Jaccard(v, u) + ε) / Σ_{w ∈ N(v)} (Jaccard(v, w) + ε), where ε is a small positive constant (e.g., 0.01). This ensures that all neighbors retain a non-zero transition probability, allowing the walk to explore the entire graph.
In addition to the Jaccard Coefficient, alternative similarity metrics will be explored, including:
-   *Adamic-Adar Index [6]: AA(v, u) = Σ_{z ∈ N(v) ∩ N(u)} 1 / log\|N(z)\|, which weights common neighbors by the inverse logarithm of their degree, giving higher importance to common neighbors with fewer connections. -   Resource Allocation Index [8]: RA(v, u) = Σ_{z ∈ N(v) ∩ N(u)} 1 / \|N(z)\|*, similar to Adamic-Adar but with a stronger penalization of high-degree common neighbors.
*Stage 2: Node Embedding Learning via Skip-gram*
The similarity-weighted random walks are then used as input sequences to the Skip-gram model, following the same approach as DeepWalk [3]. The Skip-gram model, originally proposed for learning word embeddings in natural language processing, is adapted to learn node embeddings by treating each random walk as a "sentence" and each node as a "word." The model is trained to maximize the probability of observing the context nodes (nodes within a sliding window) given the current node, resulting in dense vector representations that capture the co-occurrence patterns in the walks.
The implementation will use the Word2Vec module from the *gensim library, with configurable hyperparameters including embedding dimension d (typically 64 or 128), context window size w (typically 5 or 10), number of walks per node γ (typically 10-80), and walk length l* (typically 40-80).
*Stage 3: Link Prediction via Binary Classification*
For the link prediction task, the node embeddings are used to compute edge features for each pair of nodes. Following common practice in the literature [2][7], the edge feature for a pair (u, v) is computed using element-wise operations on the node embedding vectors, such as the Hadamard product (f_u ⊙ f_v), concatenation, or the L2 distance. These edge features are then fed into a *Logistic Regression* classifier to predict whether a link exists between the two nodes.
The model is trained on a set of positive examples (existing edges) and negative examples (randomly sampled non-edges), and evaluated using the *AUC-ROC* metric, which measures the model's ability to rank true links higher than non-links [8].
(b) Please explain the reasons why the methods, strategies, theories stated above are adopted, indicate the reference to the literature you have read so far and give your comments on the literature.
The choice of methods and strategies in this research is motivated by a careful review of the existing literature on link prediction and network representation learning.
*Network Representation Learning as the Foundation.* The adoption of network representation learning as the primary framework is motivated by its demonstrated superiority over traditional hand-crafted feature methods in capturing complex network patterns. Perozzi et al. [3] introduced DeepWalk, which pioneered the use of random walks combined with Skip-gram for learning node embeddings. Their work showed that treating random walks as analogous to sentences in natural language enables the application of powerful language modeling techniques to graph data. Grover and Leskovec [2] extended this idea with Node2Vec, introducing biased random walks that can interpolate between BFS-like (capturing structural equivalence) and DFS-like (capturing community structure) exploration strategies. Their experiments demonstrated that the flexibility of the walk strategy significantly impacts the quality of the learned embeddings. Tang et al. [4] proposed LINE, which learns embeddings by directly optimizing objective functions based on first-order and second-order proximity, offering an alternative to random walk-based approaches. These three methods form the baseline of our study, as specified by our supervisor.
*The Importance of Walk Strategy. A key insight from the literature is that the quality of random walks directly affects the quality of node embeddings. Node2Vec [2] demonstrated this by showing that different walk strategies (controlled by parameters p and q*) lead to significantly different embeddings and task performance. However, Node2Vec's bias is based only on the previous step in the walk (whether to return, stay local, or move outward), without considering the structural properties of the candidate next-hop nodes. Our proposed method takes this further by incorporating node-level structural similarity into the transition probability, which provides a more principled and information-rich bias mechanism.
*Structural Similarity Metrics.* The choice of Jaccard Coefficient as the primary weighting metric is motivated by its well-established theoretical properties and its successful application in link prediction. Liben-Nowell and Kleinberg [5] provided a comprehensive study of topology-based link prediction methods, showing that simple neighborhood-based metrics can achieve surprisingly good prediction accuracy. Adamic and Adar [6] proposed the Adamic-Adar Index, which improves upon Common Neighbors by weighting each common neighbor by the inverse logarithm of its degree. Zhou et al. [8] introduced the concept of unbiased evaluation of link prediction algorithms and proposed the Resource Allocation Index. Kumar et al. [7] provided a comprehensive survey of link prediction techniques, categorizing them into similarity-based, probabilistic, and learning-based approaches, and highlighted that the integration of multiple types of information (structural, attribute, temporal) remains an open challenge.
*Comments on the Literature.* The existing literature reveals a clear dichotomy between structural similarity-based methods and embedding-based methods for link prediction. Structural similarity methods [5][6][8] are interpretable and computationally efficient but limited to local information. Embedding methods [2][3][4] capture richer global information but do not explicitly leverage the well-established structural similarity theory. The epidemic routing framework proposed by Vahdat and Becker [1] for partially connected ad hoc networks provides an additional perspective on information diffusion in networks, which is conceptually related to the random walk process in our method. Our research aims to bridge this gap by integrating structural similarity information into the embedding generation process, creating a method that benefits from both paradigms.
(c) Please describe how you intend to validate your solution/experimental results/simulations/procedures as well as your implementation plan (e.g., programming languages, software, hardware, etc.).
*Experimental Validation Protocol*
The proposed method will be validated through comprehensive experiments on multiple real-world social network datasets. The experimental protocol follows the standard link prediction evaluation framework established in the literature [5][8].
*Datasets:*
-   *Facebook Social Network: A large-scale social network dataset containing friendship connections among Facebook users. This dataset is characterized by high clustering coefficients and strong community structure, making it an ideal testbed for methods that leverage local structural information. -   Cora: An academic citation network where nodes represent papers and edges represent citation relationships. The dataset includes 2,708 nodes and 5,429 edges, and is widely used as a benchmark in network analysis research. -   Citeseer:* Another academic citation network with 3,312 nodes and 4,732 edges, providing a complementary benchmark with different structural characteristics.
*Evaluation Methodology:*
1.  For each dataset, *20% of the existing edges are randomly removed and held out as the positive test set. An equal number of non-existing edges are randomly sampled as the negative test set. 2.  The remaining 80% of edges constitute the training graph, on which the random walks and node embeddings are generated. 3.  Edge features for each test pair are computed from the learned node embeddings using the Hadamard product. 4.  A Logistic Regression classifier is trained on a separate set of training edges and evaluated on the test set. 5.  The primary evaluation metric is the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC-ROC)*, which provides a threshold-independent measure of classification performance [8].
*Comparative Experiments:*
The following baseline methods will be implemented and compared:
-   *Traditional similarity methods: Common Neighbors, Jaccard Coefficient, Adamic-Adar Index, Resource Allocation Index. -   Network embedding methods: Original DeepWalk [3], original Node2Vec [2]. -   Proposed method:* Jaccard-weighted DeepWalk, Jaccard-weighted Node2Vec.
Each experiment will be repeated *10 times* with different random splits to ensure statistical reliability, and the mean AUC along with standard deviation will be reported.
*Implementation Plan:*
The entire experimental pipeline will be implemented in *Python* using the following libraries:
-   *networkx: For graph construction, manipulation, and computation of structural similarity metrics. -   gensim (Word2Vec): For training Skip-gram models on random walk sequences to obtain node embeddings. -   scikit-learn: For Logistic Regression classification and AUC-ROC evaluation. -   NumPy/Pandas: For data processing and result analysis. -   Matplotlib/Seaborn:* For visualization of experimental results.

(e) Please describe the novelty of the research
The primary novelty of this research lies in the *integration of local structural similarity metrics into the random walk process of network representation learning*, a direction that has received limited attention in the existing literature. The specific novel contributions are as follows:
*1. Similarity-Weighted Random Walk Strategy. The most significant contribution is the design of a new random walk strategy that replaces the uniform transition probability in DeepWalk [3] with a Jaccard Coefficient-weighted transition probability. Unlike the existing biased walk in Node2Vec [2], which controls the walk direction based on the relationship between the previous, current, and next nodes (using parameters p and q), our approach biases the walk based on the structural similarity* between the current node and each candidate next-hop neighbor. This is a fundamentally different bias mechanism that encodes richer structural information into the walk sequences. While Node2Vec's bias captures the macro-level exploration strategy (local vs. global), our similarity-based bias captures the micro-level structural affinity between adjacent nodes.
*2. Bridging Two Paradigms.* This research provides a novel bridge between the traditional topology-based link prediction methods [5][6][8] and modern network embedding methods [2][3][4]. Rather than treating these as competing approaches, we demonstrate that structural similarity information can be seamlessly integrated into the embedding generation pipeline, creating a synergistic framework that leverages the strengths of both paradigms. This integration occurs at the walk generation stage, which is the most natural point of injection as it directly determines the node co-occurrence patterns that the Skip-gram model learns from.
*3. Comprehensive Empirical Investigation.* This research provides a systematic empirical investigation of the impact of different structural similarity metrics on the quality of random walk-based node embeddings. By comparing Jaccard Coefficient, Adamic-Adar Index, and Resource Allocation Index as walk weighting functions, we provide insights into which types of structural information are most beneficial for network embedding and link prediction. This comparative analysis is itself a contribution, as it offers practical guidance for researchers and practitioners working with network embedding methods.
*4. Practical Implications.* The proposed method is designed to be a practical, drop-in improvement for existing random walk-based network embedding frameworks. It requires minimal modification to the existing codebase (only the walk generation function needs to be changed), does not introduce new neural network architectures or training procedures, and is compatible with all downstream tasks that rely on node embeddings. This makes it easy to adopt in real-world applications.
